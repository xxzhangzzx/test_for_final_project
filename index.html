San Diego climate story

Is Sunny San Diego Really Stable?

San Diego is often imagined as warm, sunny, and predictable. This project tests that idea by moving from California-wide temperature extremes to coastal signals like ocean temperature, sea level height, and precipitation.

Why extremes matter

The big picture: California extremes are shifting

San Diego is often described as having stable, warm, and sunny weather. As UCSD students, this local reputation motivated us to investigate whether that perception still holds when we look at long-term temperature data and future climate projections.

Using CMIP6 climate model data, we compare annual minimum, mean, and maximum temperatures across historical records and future emissions scenarios. Instead of only asking whether average temperature is rising, we also ask how the hottest and coldest extremes are changing.

01

California is not one climate.

California contains coastal areas, inland valleys, mountains, and deserts, so climate characteristics vary greatly from place to place. Coastal areas are moderated by the ocean, while inland and desert regions can experience much stronger temperature fluctuations.

02

Extremes matter more than averages.

Average temperature is useful, but it can hide stronger heat waves, warmer nights, or larger differences between daytime highs and nighttime lows. Maximum and minimum temperatures reveal changes that can be missed by annual averages alone.

03

Then we zoom into San Diego.

San Diego is a coastal case study. Its climate is milder than many inland regions, but local coastal conditions can still change through shifts in sea surface temperature, sea level height, and precipitation.

Coastal transition

From statewide extremes to the coast

If the temperature story shows that “stable San Diego” is more complicated than it seems, the coastal data adds another layer: ocean temperature, sea level height, and precipitation do not move in the same way every year.

During El Niño years, warmer-than-average water can pool along the eastern Pacific, pushing sea surface temperatures up and sea levels higher as the ocean expands. La Niña often reverses the pattern with cooler water, lower sea levels, and drier conditions.

Final takeaways

What the patterns reveal

01

“Stable” depends on what we measure.

San Diego may feel consistent day to day, but the data shows variation across temperature extremes, ocean temperature, sea level height, and precipitation.

02

Emissions shape the future range.

The high-emissions scenario shows stronger warming by the end of the century, especially in annual mean temperatures and hotter upper extremes.

03

The coast adds another layer.

Coastal variables do not move together perfectly. Their differences reveal a more complex climate story than temperature alone.
