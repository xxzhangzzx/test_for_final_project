Is Sunny San Diego Really Stable?

San Diego is often imagined as warm, sunny, and predictable. But climate data shows that stability depends on what we measure: temperature extremes, coastal ocean conditions, precipitation, and sea level all tell different parts of the story.

The big picture: California extremes are shifting

San Diego is often described as having stable, warm, and sunny weather. As UCSD students, this local reputation motivated us to investigate whether that perception still holds when we look at long-term temperature data and future climate projections. This project questions that motivation by looking at temperature extremes across California before zooming into San Diego’s coastal climate.

Using CMIP6 climate model data, we compare annual minimum, mean, and maximum temperatures across historical records and future emissions scenarios. Instead of only asking whether average temperature is rising, we are also asking how the hottest and coldest extremes are changing too.

California is not one climate.

California contains many different geographic regions, including coastal areas, inland areas, mountains, valleys, and deserts, so climate characteristics can vary greatly from place to place. Coastal areas are usually moderated by the ocean, which makes temperature changes relatively mild. In contrast, inland and desert regions may experience much stronger temperature fluctuations. As shown in the visualization, the temperature range in desert areas is significantly larger than that in coastal areas. Because of this, treating California as one single climate region would hide many important regional differences. This visualization helps us understand California’s climate diversity. Since much of the population lives along the coast, people may mistakenly assume that California as a whole has a warm and mild climate. However, from a broader perspective, California’s climate cannot be summarized so simply. Some regions may face stronger heat, larger temperature differences, or more noticeable seasonal changes.

Extremes matter more than averages.

Average temperature is useful, but it does not fully show the impacts of climate change. In a certain year, the average temperature may look normal, but there may still be stronger heat waves, warmer nights, or a larger difference between daytime highs and nighttime lows. For example, a day with a high of 25°C and a low of 15°C has the same average temperature as a day with a high of 40°C and a low of 0°C, both average 20°C. However, the actual climate experience and risks are very different. These extreme conditions often have a more direct impact on people’s daily lives, ecosystems, agriculture, and infrastructure because the risks they create are more noticeable and more severe. By focusing on maximum and minimum temperatures, this visualization reveals changes that could be easily missed if we only looked at annual averages.

Now zoom into San Diego.

After observing the broader climate patterns across California, San Diego can serve as a more specific coastal case study. Because it is close to the Pacific Ocean, San Diego has a milder climate compared with many inland regions of California, but this does not mean it is unaffected by climate change. The local coastal environment may still change due to shifts in sea surface temperature, sea level height, and precipitation. By focusing on San Diego, we can connect the overall climate change trends in California to the specific environmental changes that coastal cities may experience in daily life.

From statewide extremes to the coast

If the temperature story shows that “stable San Diego” is more complicated than it seems, the coastal data adds another layer: ocean temperature, sea level height, and precipitation do not move in the same way every year.

During El Niño years, warmer-than-average water pools along the eastern Pacific, pushing sea surface temperatures up and sea levels higher as the ocean expands. Precipitation often spikes in the same years. La Niña reverses the pattern: cooler water, lower sea levels, drier conditions. But the three variables don't always peak at the same time, and the magnitude differs year to year.

What the patterns reveal

1. “Stable” depends on what we measure.

San Diego may feel consistent day to day, but the data shows variation across temperature extremes, ocean temperature, sea level height, and precipitation.

2. Emissions shape the future range.

The high-emissions scenario shows stronger warming by the end of the century, especially in annual mean temperatures and hotter upper extremes.

3. The coast adds another layer.

Coastal variables do not move together perfectly. Some years show warmer ocean temperatures, higher sea level height, or unusual rainfall, revealing a more complex climate story than temperature alone.

Future versions of this project could expand the analysis by comparing coastal, inland, mountain, and desert San Diego regions. Adding vegetation, rainfall, and ecosystem indicators would help show how climate change affects different communities and landscapes within the same county.
